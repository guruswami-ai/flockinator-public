# Authority: the commander and the platoon

Picture a rescue commander sending a swarm of scout drones to
find a lost child, the way Flockinator's panels pretend to be a
survey flock. She cannot fly each one, and smoke, terrain, and
distance keep breaking their radios. By the time a human could
understand one drone's view, sixteen drones have each made a
hundred choices.

So what is she trusting?

Not a single brilliant drone. She is trusting a **platoon**.
A platoon works because each soldier is trained to make good
decisions alone, the group is trained to make better ones
together, and everyone has been tested - in practice, in bad
weather, and against an opposing team whose job was to make
them fail.

A drone swarm asks for exactly the same trust:

| Platoon | Swarm |
|---|---|
| Each soldier decides well alone | Each node decides well on its own senses |
| The platoon decides better together | The swarm's shared picture beats any node's |
| Orders set the mission, not every step | Authority sets goals and limits, not every move |
| Trust comes from exercises and honesty about failures | Trust comes from measured runs, hostile tests, and published rejections |

That last row is why Flockinator publishes its failures: policies
that were rejected, false beliefs that won, attacks that worked.
Trust in an autonomous swarm is not a feeling. It is evidence,
collected under friendly *and* hostile conditions, that the group
does its duty when nobody can supervise it moment to moment.

One difference matters. A soldier can refuse an unlawful order
and can share responsibility for what the platoon does. A node
can do neither in any meaningful sense. Trust transfers from the
platoon analogy; blame does not - it stays with the commander
and the designers, more concentrated, not less. That asymmetry
is itself an argument for hostile testing before deployment.

The swarm must be smarter together. It must also, node by node,
still be good enough alone. We test both.
